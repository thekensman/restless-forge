LED Hologram Fans: How a Spinning Blade Draws an Image

Published March 2026 · Updated October 2026 · By Kenneth Cross

An LED hologram fan is a blade, or several, lined with LEDs and spun fast enough to disappear. As it turns, a controller switches each LED on and off at exactly the right moment, and your eye merges the sweeps into a picture that seems to hang in the air. Unlike a pyramid, a fan reflects nothing: it draws the image directly, one ring of light at a time, and that shapes what works on one.

Persistence of vision, in practice

Your eye cannot follow the blade. What it registers is the light each LED leaves along its path, blended over many turns. Once the blade completes a turn faster than your eye can separate, the trails fuse into a steady image and the dark blade between the lit points vanishes. A faster blade, or more blades, makes the picture steadier; more LEDs along each blade make it sharper.

The picture is drawn in rings and spokes

A rectangular image has to be redrawn in the fan's own geometry. Each LED sits at a fixed distance from the hub and can only ever draw one ring. The controller divides each turn into a fixed number of steps and decides, at every step, which LEDs to light. The fan's pixels sit where those rings cross those spokes.

That makes detail uneven across the disc. Near the hub the steps crowd together, and at the rim they spread apart. On a hypothetical fan that switches its LEDs 360 times per turn, the gap between steps is under 1 mm at 5 cm from the hub and about 4.4 mm at the rim of a 50 cm disc. Fine detail belongs near the centre; the edge suits bold shapes.

Black is the trick

An LED that is off is invisible, and through the gaps in the spinning blade you see the wall behind. That is what makes a fan image float: every black pixel in your file is a hole in the picture. It is also why the background must be pure black. Anything else, even a faint grey, lights LEDs across the whole disc and turns the floating object into a glowing plate.

Sending a file to a fan

Fans differ more than any other hologram display, so check the manual for three things:

  • Which formats it takes. Some fans read GIFs or videos from a memory card; others only accept files through their own app, which converts what you give it.
  • Its resolution. Fans are far coarser than an 800-pixel canvas, and their software scales your file down. If the fan's app limits file size, the Resolution setting of 256 gives a 512-pixel canvas at about half the size.
  • How it handles timing. A converter may re-time frames to the fan's own rate, so a loop can run faster or slower than it did in HoloPath.

At default settings, a 24-frame fan GIF measured 6.9 MB on a test subject. If that is too big for your fan's transfer, cut Noise first, because random grain compresses worst of all. Fewer frames or a lower resolution come next.

Mounting and viewing

A fan is a fast-spinning blade, so mount it on a wall or stand out of reach, with its guard on if it came with one. View it from straight in front: from the side the disc flattens into an ellipse and the image with it. Like every hologram display, it looks best in dim light.
