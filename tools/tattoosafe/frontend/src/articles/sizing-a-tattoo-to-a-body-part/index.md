The most common tattoo regret is not the design. It is the size.

People show their artist a picture on a phone, agree it looks great, and end up
with something noticeably smaller or larger than the thing in their head. The
design was fine. The problem was that a screen tells you nothing about how a
piece relates to a forearm.

## Why screens mislead

An image on a phone is roughly 7 cm wide, viewed at 30 cm, filling most of your
visual field. The same design on an inner forearm might be 8 cm wide on a
surface 25 cm long, seen at whatever distance people stand at.

Your brain does not do that conversion. It stores "how the design looked" —
which was: large, centred, detailed, the only thing you were looking at. On skin
it is a small object among other things, at an angle, on a curved surface.

The result is a systematic bias in one direction. People consistently
underestimate the size needed for a design to read the way it did on screen,
which is why "I wish I'd gone bigger" is a far more common regret than the
reverse.

## Bodies scale, and not the way you would guess

The other half of the problem is that "an inner forearm" is not one size.

Body proportions scale with height in roughly predictable ratios — forearm
length as a fraction of total height, upper arm as a fraction of that. A person
at 155 cm and a person at 195 cm do not just differ in height; every available
canvas differs proportionally.

This is why generic size guidance fails. "A 10 cm design suits an inner forearm"
is reasonable advice for someone of average height and actively misleading at
either extreme. On a short forearm it dominates; on a long one it floats in the
middle looking undersized.

## What actually limits size

Four constraints, in rough order of how often they bite.

**Usable area.** Each placement has boundaries that are not the whole limb.
An inner forearm runs wrist to elbow crease, but the last couple of centimetres
at each end are awkward — skin folds, the piece distorts when you bend. The
usable area is meaningfully smaller than the anatomical one.

**Curvature.** Flat designs on cylindrical limbs distort. A wide piece wrapping
an arm cannot be seen all at once, and geometric designs with straight lines
suffer badly — the eye notices a bowed line immediately. Organic designs
tolerate curvature far better, which is why so much traditional flash is
organic.

**Detail floor.** Skin is not paper. Ink spreads slightly over years, and lines
closer than roughly a millimetre tend to merge over time. Fine cross-hatching
and small text that look crisp at healing can become a grey smudge in a decade.
Scaling a detailed design down does not preserve it — it schedules its
destruction.

**Reading distance.** Something intended to be recognised across a room needs
bold shapes and high contrast. Something intended to be discovered up close can
carry detail. These are different design problems and the size decision is
downstream of which one you are solving.

## Working it out before the shop

The order that works:

**Decide reading distance first.** Across-the-room or up-close? This constrains
everything after it.

**Check fit, don't just pick a number.** Enter dimensions and see whether they
fit the placement on your proportions. TattooSafe will tell you when a design
exceeds the usable area rather than letting you discover it from a stencil.

**Use the camera view for placement, the silhouette for measurement.** The
camera preview answers "does this sit right here" — position, angle,
relationship to your actual arm. The silhouette answers "how big is this
really," in centimetres, and is the one to screenshot for your artist.

**Try one size up.** Given the systematic bias toward undersizing, deliberately
previewing a larger version is worth the ten seconds. Often it is obviously
better, and the cost difference is smaller than the regret.

**Then talk to your artist.** They will have opinions about how the design needs
to change at your chosen size — line weights that work at 15 cm do not work at
7 cm, and details often need removing rather than shrinking. That conversation
goes much better when you arrive with dimensions instead of a phone.

## What a preview cannot tell you

It is a flat overlay. It does not wrap to your arm, respond to how skin moves, or
account for how the piece will look in three years.

What it does is remove the most avoidable failure mode — discovering scale at
the moment the stencil goes on, when you have already chosen the design, booked
the slot, and paid the deposit.
