Pricing Studio Practice

How Tattoo Studios Actually Price Work

August 4, 2026 By Kenneth Cross 8 min read

Ask what a tattoo costs and you will get a question back: how big, what style, where on your body. That is not evasion. Those three answers, plus the artist's rate, are genuinely the whole calculation — and they combine in a specific order that explains most of the quotes people find surprising.

Almost nobody prices by the piece

Flash sheets sometimes carry fixed prices, and very small standard designs sometimes do. Everything else is priced by time.

This is not a pricing strategy so much as an admission of how the work goes. Tattooing is a skilled manual process on a surface that varies between people and moves while you work on it. An experienced artist can predict the hours fairly well; nobody can predict them exactly. Hourly billing puts that uncertainty where it belongs.

Area, not size

Everything starts with square inches, and this is where intuition fails first.

Doubling a design's dimensions does not double the work — it quadruples it. A 2 × 2 inch piece is 4 square inches. A 4 × 4 inch piece is 16. "Can we make it a bit bigger?" is the single most expensive casual sentence in a consultation, and it rarely feels like it should be.

Run it the other direction and it is good news: shrinking a design modestly cuts the cost sharply. If a quote is above budget, size is the most efficient lever you have.

Style is the biggest multiplier

Time per square inch varies enormously by what is actually being put into the skin:

Style Minutes per sq in
Simple line work 8
Moderate detail — shading, dotwork, traditional 15
Full colour — fills, blending, neo-traditional 25
Photorealistic / portrait 40

Five times from one end to the other. This is why the same design at the same size can be quoted at wildly different prices by artists who understood the brief differently — and why establishing style before discussing money saves everyone time.

The mechanism is passes. Line work is largely one pass with a fine needle grouping. Colour and realism mean going over the same skin repeatedly to build tone, blend edges, and let areas settle. Each square inch is worked several times over.

Placement is about difficulty, not just pain

Placement multipliers run from 1.0 to about 1.4:

  • 1.0 — upper arm, forearm, thigh, upper back. Flat, fleshy, stationary.
  • 1.05–1.15 — shoulder, calf, lower back, chest, wrist.
  • 1.2–1.3 — ankle, back of neck, behind the ear.
  • 1.35–1.4 — ribs, sternum, back of the hand.

These track how hard the surface is to work on. Ribs curve in two directions over bone that moves with every breath. Hands have thin, mobile skin over tendons. An outer thigh is a nearly flat, stable canvas.

Pain enters the calculation through a side door: it determines how long you can sit. A high-pain placement means more breaks, and breaks are billed time. So painful placements cost more twice over.

The hourly rate

Tier Typical hourly
Apprentice $80–120
Experienced (3–8 years) $120–200
Specialist / award-winning $200–350
Celebrity / high-demand $350–500+

Rate reflects demand as much as skill — a long waiting list pushes prices up independently of technique. It also does not translate directly into total cost, because faster artists finish sooner. A $250/hour specialist who completes in four hours costs less than a $150/hour artist who needs eight, and the result is usually better.

The parts of the bill that are not needle time

Setup, around 30 minutes: stencil placement and repositioning, equipment prep, the conversation where you confirm the design is right. It happens every session.

Breaks, roughly 5 minutes per hour. Necessary for both of you.

Session limits, typically 6 hours. Past that, skin swells and takes ink poorly, and both of you are tired in ways that show. Large work splits into multiple sessions — each carrying its own setup, and its own healing gap before the next.

Shop minimum, commonly $50–150. A tiny piece that takes twelve minutes still costs the minimum, because a fresh needle, fresh ink caps, sterilisation, and the booking slot do not scale down.

Why your quote came in higher

In rough order of frequency:

  1. Style classification. You called it "simple"; the artist sees shading.
  2. Cover-ups. Working over existing ink is slower than clean skin, and is consistently underestimated by everyone except the person doing it.
  3. Design time. Custom drawing is often billed separately or via deposit.
  4. Placement. Ribs and hands genuinely take longer.
  5. Region. Big-city rates can be double a small town's.

A quote well above an estimate is usually correct, and the productive question is "what about this takes longer than I'm picturing" rather than "why so expensive."

A quote well below is the one to worry about. Underpricing usually signals an apprentice, a rushed session, or corners cut somewhere you cannot see — and this is a permanent object on your body.

Using an estimate properly

An estimate is a sanity check. It tells you whether a quote is in a reasonable range, and lets you have a more informed conversation about size and style if it is not.

It is not a negotiating position. Artists are not overcharging you because you found a number on the internet, and arriving with a printout to argue from is a good way to start the relationship badly.
