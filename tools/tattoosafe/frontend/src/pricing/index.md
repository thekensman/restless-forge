Almost nobody quotes a tattoo by the piece. Artists quote by the hour, and the
number of hours comes from size, style, and where on your body the work goes.
Once you know how those three inputs turn into time, a quote stops feeling
arbitrary and you can tell a fair one from a bad one.

This page documents exactly what TattooSafe's estimator does, including the
numbers it uses, so you can check its reasoning rather than trust it.

## The chain: area to time to money

Everything starts with **area**, not with the longest dimension. A tattoo twice
as wide and twice as tall is four times the work, which is why "just make it a
bit bigger" changes a quote so much more than people expect.

Area in square inches is multiplied by a **time-per-square-inch** rate for the
style, then by a **placement multiplier**, giving needle time. Setup time and
breaks are added. Finally, hours are multiplied by the artist's hourly rate.

## Style: the largest single multiplier

| Style | Minutes per sq in |
|---|---|
| Simple line work — outlines, script, minimal shading | 8 |
| Moderate detail — shading, dotwork, small fills, traditional | 15 |
| Full colour — colour fills, blending, neo-traditional | 25 |
| Photorealistic / portrait — hyper-detail, cover-ups | 40 |

The spread from simple line work to photorealism is **five times**. This single
choice moves a quote more than anything else, which is why an artist's first
question is usually about style rather than size.

The reason is packing density. Line work lays down one pass. Colour and realism
require repeated passes over the same skin — building tone, blending edges,
letting areas rest — so each square inch is worked several times over.

## Placement: difficulty, not just pain

Placement carries a multiplier from 1.0 to 1.4:

| Multiplier | Placements |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | Upper arm, inner/outer forearm, thigh, upper back |
| 1.05 | Shoulder, calf, lower back |
| 1.1–1.15 | Chest, wrist |
| 1.2 | Ankle, back of neck |
| 1.3 | Behind ear |
| 1.35 | Ribs, sternum |
| 1.4 | Back of hand |

These track working difficulty, which correlates with pain but is not the same
thing. Ribs and sternum are slow because the surface curves in two directions
over bone that moves with every breath. Hands are the highest multiplier because
the skin is thin, mobile, and full of tendons. A flat, fleshy, stationary
surface like an outer thigh is simply faster to work on.

Pain matters for a different reason: it determines how long you can sit. A
high-pain placement means more breaks, and breaks are billed time.

## Artist rate

| Tier | Hourly |
|---|---|
| Apprentice (learning) | $80–120 |
| Experienced (3–8 yrs) | $120–200 |
| Specialist / award-winning | $200–350 |
| Celebrity / high-demand | $350–500+ |

Rate is not a proxy for quality alone — it reflects demand and waiting list as
much as skill. A specialist may also be faster, so a higher hourly rate does not
always mean a proportionally higher total.

## Sessions, setup, and breaks

Estimated time is not just needle time:

- **Setup** adds a flat 30 minutes: stencil placement, positioning, equipment.
- **Breaks** add roughly 5 minutes per hour.
- **Sessions cap at 6 hours.** Past that, work is split — skin swells, and both
  of you get tired in ways that show in the result.

Multi-session work usually costs slightly more in total than the same hours in
one sitting, because each session carries its own setup and its own healing gap.

## Shop minimums

Most shops have a minimum charge, typically **$50–150**, regardless of how small
the piece is. A one-inch symbol that takes twelve minutes still costs the
minimum. This covers a fresh needle, fresh ink caps, sterilised equipment, and
the booking slot — real costs that do not scale down.

If your estimate lands below the minimum, expect to pay the minimum.

## What the estimate does not include

- **Design time.** Custom drawing is often billed separately or as a deposit.
- **Touch-ups.** Many artists include one free; some do not.
- **Tip.** Customary in many places, typically 15–20%.
- **Regional variation.** Rates in a major city can be double a small town's.
- **Cover-ups and reworks.** These are consistently underestimated — working
  over existing ink is slower than working on clean skin.

## Using an estimate well

Treat the number as a range to sanity-check a quote, not as a price to negotiate
toward. If a quote comes in far above the estimate, the useful question is not
"why so expensive" but "what about this piece takes longer than I'm picturing" —
the answer is usually complexity or placement, and it is usually correct.

If a quote comes in far *below*, be careful. Underpricing tends to mean an
apprentice, a rushed session, or a shop cutting corners somewhere you cannot
see.
