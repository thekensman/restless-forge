Almost everyone knows their salary. Almost nobody knows what an hour of their
working life is actually worth, because the two numbers are separated by a
surprising amount of arithmetic.

The usual shortcut is salary ÷ 2,080 — fifty-two weeks of forty hours. It is
wrong in both directions at once: it overstates the money by ignoring taxes and
job costs, and it understates the hours by counting only the ones on your
contract. This article walks through every subtraction the calculator makes and
why each one belongs.

## Step one: what actually reaches your account

Three deductions come out before anything else, and they behave differently.

**Federal income tax** is charged in brackets, and this is where most mental
arithmetic goes wrong. Being "in the 22% bracket" does not mean 22% of your
income goes to federal tax — it means the *last* dollar you earned was taxed at
22%. The dollars below it were taxed at 10% and 12%. The calculator walks the
brackets properly, applying each rate only to the income that falls inside it,
which is why your effective rate always lands well below your marginal rate.

**State income tax** varies from nothing at all to over 13%. It is the single
largest source of variation between two otherwise identical salaries in
different places.

**FICA** — Social Security and Medicare — behaves unlike the others. Medicare
applies to every dollar you earn. Social Security stops at a wage base, above
which those dollars are exempt. This creates a genuinely counterintuitive
effect: your marginal tax rate can *fall* as you cross that threshold. If you
have ever noticed a late-year paycheck arriving slightly larger for no obvious
reason, this is usually why.

## Step two: the money the job costs you

A job is not free to hold. The calculator subtracts what you spend because you
work, not what you spend generally:

- **Commuting** — fuel, transit fares, parking, the share of vehicle wear
  attributable to the trip.
- **Work clothing** — anything you buy because the job requires it.
- **Meals** — the marginal cost of lunches out versus eating at home.
- **Childcare** — often the largest single item, and frequently the one that
  decides whether a second income is worth holding at all.
- **Other work-driven costs** — tools, licences, certifications, professional
  memberships.

The commute and meal figures are multiplied by your *actual* working days —
52 weeks times your days per week, minus vacation, holidays, and sick days. This
matters more than it sounds: someone with a four-day week and generous leave has
substantially fewer commutes to pay for than the naive 260-day assumption
implies.

## Step three: the hours nobody counts

This is where the number really moves, and where most other calculators stop
short.

Your contracted hours are only part of what the job consumes. The calculator
also counts:

- **Commute time**, both directions, every working day.
- **Getting ready** — the portion you would not spend if you were not going to
  work.
- **Decompression** — the time after work when you are home but not yet
  yourself. This is the most contested input and the most honest one. If you
  need forty minutes of staring at a wall before you can cook dinner, the job
  took that time.
- **Unpaid overtime** — the evenings and weekends that never appear on a
  timesheet.

Every one of these hours is unpaid, and every one is unavailable for anything
else. Excluding them is how a job that eats eleven hours of your day gets to
call itself an eight-hour job.

## Putting it together

The real hourly wage is take-home pay minus work costs, divided by *all* the
hours the job consumes:

```
(annual take-home − annual work costs) ÷ (contracted + commute
  + prep + decompression + unpaid overtime hours)
```

Both halves move against you compared with the naive version. The numerator
shrinks; the denominator grows. It is common for the result to land at half the
salary-divided-by-2,080 figure, and for people with long commutes or heavy
childcare costs it can be worse than that.

## What to do with the number

The point is not to be depressed by it. The point is that it makes previously
incomparable things comparable.

**Purchases become hours.** A $600 item at a $22 real hourly wage costs about 27
hours — most of a working week. Whether that is worth it is your call, but it is
a different question from whether $600 is affordable.

**Job changes become clearer.** A raise that adds an hour of daily commuting can
easily be a downgrade. The calculator will tell you which way it actually lands.

**Trade-offs get priced.** Moving closer to work, dropping to four days,
negotiating remote time — each converts into a real change in what your hour is
worth, and can be compared against the salary change it costs you.

**And the reverse.** If the number comes out *higher* than you expected, that is
useful too. It means the job costs you less than it feels like, and the thing
making you tired is probably not the commute.
